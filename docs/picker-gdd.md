# Alpha-Chain: "Picker" Mode — Game Design Document

> **Scope.** This document specifies **Picker**, a second play mode for Alpha-Chain, and the
> **Preference Card** family that supports it. It describes **this repository's** build and is
> authoritative for the mode.
>
> **On the base GDD.** Rules Picker does not change are still described by `alpha-chain-gdd.md` in
> the **archived** .NET original (`D:\C# Projects\KnockBox\host\KnockBox.AlphaChain\Specs\`), and
> references written `(base §2.2)` point there for background. That document is reliable for the
> game's *rules and architecture* but **not** for its card catalogue, which this build has moved
> well past (47 cards vs. the documented 40). Where this document names a card's numbers, they are
> **this build's**, read from `src/game/cards/library.ts`. See §8.

---

## 1. Overview

**Picker** replaces word entry with word *selection*. On their turn a player is shown a set of
candidate words — **the Offer** — and chooses one. Everything downstream is unchanged: the chosen
word runs through the player's Engine Bay exactly as a typed word does, sets the next player's
Succession letter, and is subject to the Zero-Point Tax.

Classic Alpha-Chain asks two things of a player: **recall** a word starting with a given letter, and
**type it before the clock dies**. Both are barriers. Recall rewards vocabulary breadth over
strategy, and fast typing is a hard wall for dyslexic players and for the mobile users who make up
most of the audience.

Picker trades those for a different skill: **evaluation**. A word's score is a function of its
*shape* — its length, its vowel and consonant counts, its distinct letters, its rare letters, its
repeats, whether its first and last letters match. The Engine Bay is a machine that pays for
particular shapes. Reading five words and knowing which one your machine pays best for is a real
skill, and it is one that improves with engine knowledge rather than with dictionary knowledge.

The consequence is that **the engine becomes the primary skill expression.** In Classic a strong
player can dig for a great word; in Picker your ceiling is what you were dealt, and the way you
raise that ceiling is by building a better machine. This is intended.

### 1.1 Picker as the Default

**Picker is the default mode.** Classic remains available for players who want the typing race.
Because Picker is the default rather than an alternate, several things that would otherwise be
follow-up work are part of the mode's definition:

*   **The tutorial must teach Picker.** The era-1 walkthrough currently covers typed entry
    (`TutorialKind` = `shiritori`, `timeout`, `engine`, `cards`, `tax`, `sniper`). A first-run player
    now needs an **Offer** tutorial in place of `shiritori`, and `timeout` changes meaning entirely
    (§2.4). Classic keeps the existing walkthrough.
*   **The disabled-card list defines the default deal pool** (§4.4). Cards cut from Picker become
    Classic-only, which changes the pool size behind the lobby's deal-capacity warning.
*   **Bots must play Picker at launch**, because solo-vs-bots is the default first-run experience.
*   **Classic must stay discoverable** in the lobby, since stored settings will load with Picker
    selected.

## 2. The Word Picker

### 2.1 The Offer

*   **The Offer** is a set of candidate words presented to the active player. Each entry is an
    **Offer Card**. The count is host-configurable, default **5**.
*   **The Offer is public.** Every player sees the active player's candidates. Turns are sequential,
    so exactly **one** Offer is on screen at any moment — there is no per-player hidden state, and the
    server stays in broadcast mode (§7).
*   **Everything is visible at once.** The Offer renders as a wrapping grid whose card size scales to
    the count — one row on desktop, two on a phone. Offer Cards must never be scrolled off-screen:
    an option you have to go looking for is hidden information under a shot clock, which is exactly
    the friction Picker exists to remove. Horizontal scrolling is a fallback beyond **8** cards only.
*   **Selection is a distinct state from submission.** A tap or click **selects** an Offer Card; a
    second tap, or the GO button, **commits** it. Touch devices have no hover, so selection must be
    explicit — and selection is where the reading aids in §2.3 live.

### 2.2 Offer Generation

The Offer is generated by the **Word Picker**, server-side, once per turn.

*   **The Offer is not a uniform draw.** The shipped word list is severely length-skewed — median
    word length **9**, with **73%** of entries at 8+ letters and **45%** at 10+. A uniform draw of 5
    words would contain a 10+ letter word roughly **95%** of turns, which would make Sesquipedalian
    (×5 at 10+ letters) permanently active and hand every player a large base score for free. Since
    the scoring seed **is** the word length (base §5), an unshaped Offer would break the game's
    entire length economy. The picker draws against a **corrected length distribution** instead.
*   **Ending-letter lookahead.** Because the Succession letter is the last letter of the chosen word
    (base §2.1), the picker implicitly controls the chain's letter graph. It will not offer a word
    whose ending letter would leave the next player a starved pool, given the active Preference Cards
    and the words already used. This is what keeps the chain healthy deep into a match, and it is
    what makes the Reduced dictionary (below) viable at all — without it, a chain ending in X or Z on
    a 9,884-word list can leave a player with three legal words they have already seen.
*   **The Offer always contains at least one legal word** — one that satisfies Succession and has not
    already been played this match. Legality here means *playable*, not *safe*.
*   **The Offer does not guarantee a clean word, and never marks a dirty one.** Words carrying the
    era Banned Letter, a personal ban, or a hijack appear in the Offer unannounced and score **0**
    when the Zero-Point Tax fires (base §2.2). Discovering that the biggest word on the board was
    poisoned only when the score lands is intended. See §5 for the accessibility exception.
*   Already-played words are excluded. Bans are never a generation filter — only a scoring
    consequence.
*   **Dictionary Selection** is a host setting with two pools:
    *   **Full** — the complete **386,633**-word list. Maximum variety, heavily obscure.
    *   **Reduced** — the **9,884**-word common-English list. Its value is not fairness but
        **decodability**: a reader recognises CANDLE at a glance and must decode ZYGODACTYL letter by
        letter, and whole-word recognition is precisely what dyslexia impairs. The cost is
        repetition across matches, which is why it is a setting and not the only pool.

### 2.3 Reading an Offer

*   **Offer Cards are annotated with the features the engine scores on** — letter count, vowel count,
    rare letters, whether the word ends in a vowel. The annotations are the mode's most important
    accessibility affordance: they let a player **evaluate an Offer Card without fully reading the
    word**. You can play by shape.
*   **Selecting an Offer Card highlights the Engine Bay cards that would fire for it.** This teaches
    the machine without solving it, and it is the primary way a new player learns what their engine
    wants.
*   **The projected score is never shown.** A displayed number turns the decision into a lookup — the
    player stops evaluating and clicks the biggest figure. The uncertainty is the game. Cards that
    interact positionally (Magnifying Glass, Forgery, the Catalyst) make an exact mental total
    genuinely hard, and that is a feature: the decision resolves as a judgement call, not arithmetic.

### 2.4 Timeout Behaviour

*   When the shot clock expires, the Picker **commits the player's current selection**. If no Offer
    Card is selected, it commits one **at random**.
*   **There is no timeout point penalty in Picker.** The clock enforces *pace*, not punishment — a
    slow reader is never docked for reading slowly, which would reintroduce the barrier the mode
    exists to remove. The base timeout penalty and every card's timeout drain are inert in Picker
    (§4.4).
*   A clock expiry with **no selection made** is a **no-show**. The random pick still resolves so the
    chain continues, but Survival mode (base §6) eliminates on the no-show, not on the timeout —
    otherwise Survival could never eliminate anyone in Picker.
*   The shot clock duration is host-configurable. Its default is a **playtest question**, not a design
    decision: too generous and turns drag, which is fatal in a party game; too tight and Picker
    becomes a reading-speed race.

## 3. Preference Cards

**Preference Cards** are a new family that shapes the Offer instead of scoring the word. They are
**FX** cards — scoring-inert, base ×1.0 — and they are dealt from the same pool, at the same
Intermission, as every other Modifier.

**Preference Cards occupy Engine Bay slots.** There is no second engine. This is the design's load
bearing decision: a separate picker engine would be a strip of pure upside, and pure upside is not a
decision. Sharing the bay converts the whole family into an extension of the **Intermission Dilemma**
(base §3) and poses a question with no correct answer — *do I want the highest possible ceiling, or do
I want to reliably be offered good words?*

It also defuses a failure the family would otherwise have. A Preference Card that simply biased the
Offer toward what your scoring cards already like would hand you five near-identical good words and
collapse the choice: the better your picker aligned with your engine, the less there would be to
decide. Slot competition prevents that alignment from ever being free.

**Design guardrail.** Preference Cards are **shape constraints with a cost**, not alignment buffs.
*"Only words of 6+ letters"* is a poor bonus and an excellent **Glass Cannon** (base §3.3) — it means
you can never duck a Banned Letter with a short safe word. Any Preference Card that is strictly good
for its owner is mis-designed.

### 3.1 Placement and Bubbling

*   Preference Cards **auto-bubble to the leftmost position** that is not already a Preference Card.
    A player cannot place one in the middle of their scoring chain.
*   This is not cosmetic. The Magnifying Glass magnifies the card **immediately to its right**; a
    manually-placed Preference Card would silently waste it. Bubbling makes the interaction
    impossible rather than punishing.
*   The side effect is a clean visual split — the Preference block on the left, the scoring chain on
    the right — without a second bay.
*   Preference Cards do **not** shift the relative order of scoring cards, so bubbling never changes
    what a word scores.

### 3.2 Composition and Unsatisfiable Filters

*   Preference Cards **compose left → right**, matching the Catalyst and Forgery idiom (base §3.8).
    Their order relative to each other is player-controlled and meaningful; filters intersect.
*   **A filter that cannot be satisfied is skipped**, so the picker can never soft-lock. Filters are
    applied left to right and any filter that would drop the candidate pool below the configured
    Offer count is skipped, not partially applied — the Offer is always served at full size.
*   Skipping must be **deterministic**. Offer generation runs inside the server authority under an
    injected RNG; a filter resolution that varied between runs would desynchronise multiplayer.

### 3.3 Interaction with Scoring

*   Preference Cards are **invisible to bay-size scoring.** They do not count toward the bay length or
    the "cards to the right" count. Without this rule, **Dividend** (+2 per card in your bay) and
    **Booster Pack** (+2 per card to its right, scaled by slot count) would both silently inflate —
    and Booster Pack doubly so, because bubbling puts every Preference Card to its left.
*   They fold nothing and are never magnified. A Magnifying Glass can never target one (§3.1).
*   They do not occupy the perceived-length, letter-classification or clock chains, so Forgery, the
    Catalyst and the Glass Cannon clock maths are unaffected by their presence.

### 3.4 The Initial Set

Seven cards. Each pays for its effect with an Engine Bay slot, and most with something else besides.

*   **The Sieve** — **FX**. Your Offer contains only words of **6+ letters**. You will never be
    offered a short word to duck a Banned Letter with.
*   **The Winnower** — **FX**. Once per turn, redraw your entire Offer at a cost of **30%** of your
    armed shot clock. The price is fixed, so it grows harsher as your engine grows and each Offer
    takes longer to read.
*   **The Wide Net** — **FX**. **+2** Offer Cards, and **−15%** shot clock. More to choose from, less
    time to choose.
*   **Tunnel Vision** — **FX**, ×1.4 always. **−2** Offer Cards. The purest statement of the mode's
    dilemma: raw multiplier bought with selection quality.
*   **The Prospector** — **FX**. At least one Offer Card always contains a rare letter (**Q, X, Z,
    J**). One of your slots is permanently spent on a word you may not want.
*   **The Tide** — **FX**. Your Offer is drawn vowel-heavy where the pool allows. The cost is
    concentration: a narrower pool means more repeated words and a thinner ending-letter graph for
    the picker to work with (§2.2).
*   **The Sentinel** — **FX**. At least one Offer Card is guaranteed clean of every Banned Letter
    active against you. Insurance against the Zero-Point Tax, paid for in slots — and it spends an
    Offer slot on safety rather than on ceiling.

## 4. Changes to Existing Systems

### 4.1 Succession and The Wildcard

Succession is unchanged as a rule, but the player no longer *satisfies* it — the picker does, by
only offering words that start with the required letter (§2.2).

*   **The Wildcard** is reframed. Instead of *"one word per era may ignore Succession"*, it becomes
    *"once per era, your Offer ignores the required letter."* Same charge, same per-era re-arm, same
    guard state. In Picker this is a genuine power: a free-letter Offer is drawn from the whole pool
    rather than from one letter's slice, which matters most on exactly the starved letters the
    picker's lookahead is otherwise steering around.

### 4.2 The Zero-Point Tax

The Tax is unchanged mechanically and is **more visible** than in Classic, because the poisoned word
is something you were handed rather than something you typed.

One property is worth stating plainly: in Classic, eating the Tax is a deliberate tactical pass (base
§2.2) — you choose to burn a turn to shed a bad letter. In Picker that choice only exists when the
Offer happens to contain a taxed word. This is an accepted reduction in agency, traded for the
comedy of picking a poisoned word unaware.

**Slow Burn** interacts specifically. Its sub-6-letter rule is a *legality* tax, not a Banned Letter,
so the Prism does not rescue it (§4.3) — and the picker does not filter for legality. A Slow Burn
owner will therefore be offered short, illegal words unless they also hold **The Sieve**, which makes
that pairing genuinely strong and worth watching in balance.

### 4.3 The Prism

**The Prism is unchanged and is not repurposed.** Its clock-rescue already fits Picker better than it
fits Classic: on a submission carrying a Banned Letter it rejects the word and refills the clock, which
in Picker means *"you picked the poisoned one — here is your Offer back."* It is a reroll triggered by
the mode's funniest moment, and it needs no redesign.

Its timeout half is nearly inert in Picker, since timeouts auto-commit (§2.4). The banned-letter half
becomes its primary mode.

### 4.4 Disabled and Retuned Cards

**Disabled in Picker** (dealt only in Classic):

*   **The Blindfold** — masks the player's own input box. There is no input box.
*   **Insurance** — negates the timeout point penalty. Picker has no timeout penalty (§2.4).

**Requires retuning.** Picker changes the clock economy: committing a selection is far faster than
typing a word, so every card reading "time left on the clock" pays out closer to its ceiling, and
every card whose downside is a timeout drain loses that downside entirely.

*   **Panic Button**, **Speedracer** — multipliers scaling with remaining clock. Both approach their
    caps routinely in Picker. Retune the curves, not the caps.
*   **Chrono Syphon** — banks per whole second left on an *opponent's* clock. Fast commits inflate
    this substantially; it is the most affected card in the catalogue.
*   **The Vault**, **Redline** — their clock costs still bite, because evaluation genuinely takes
    time, but their timeout drains never fire. Both are net **buffed** in Picker and should be
    re-costed.
*   **The Sniper** — its leader clock-shave is stronger in Picker, since clock pressure scales with
    how long an Offer takes to evaluate. No change expected, but it is the one aggression card whose
    value moves.

Unaffected and explicitly checked: **Scavenger** and **The Blueprint** (read submission history and
previous word length, both intact), the entire tax and siphon economy (base §3.5), and every card
whose trigger is purely a property of the word's shape.

## 5. Accessibility

Picker exists for two audiences the Classic loop serves badly.

**Dyslexic players.** Classic asks for recall *and* rapid typing. Picker removes both. But it is
worth being honest that Picker replaces them with a reading task, and reading is not free for the
players this mode is for. Three design elements answer that directly, and they are requirements
rather than polish:

*   The **feature annotations** on Offer Cards (§2.3), which allow evaluation without full decoding.
*   The **Reduced dictionary** (§2.2), which restores whole-word recognition.
*   The **Offer count setting**, which is a cognitive-load dial as much as a strategic one.

*   **Banned-letter highlighting** — a setting, **off by default**. When on, occurrences of an active
    Banned Letter are highlighted inside Offer words. Scanning several words for one letter under a
    clock is close to the hardest single operation you can ask of a dyslexic reader, and it sits on
    the mechanic that zeroes their score. The HUD already publishes the Banned Letter, so the toggle
    leaks nothing the game has not already told everyone; it is a rendering change with no rules,
    balance, or network effect. Competitive tables leave it off and keep the surprise of §2.2.

**Mobile players.** Typing a long word accurately on a phone keyboard, against a shot clock, is a
worse experience than the desktop version of the same task by a wide margin. Selection is native to
touch. The layout rule in §2.1 — everything visible, never scrolled — is what makes this hold on a
small screen.

## 6. Host Configuration

Extends base §7.

*   **Game Mode:** **Picker** (default) | Classic
*   **Offer Count:** number of Offer Cards per turn. Default **5**.
*   **Offer Dictionary:** Full (386,633 words) | Reduced (9,884 common words)
*   **Highlight Banned Letters:** Off (default) | On
*   **Picker Shot Clock:** time to select a word. Distinct from the Classic shot clock, because the
    right value for reading is not the right value for typing. Default set by playtest.

## 7. Implementation Notes

*   **Offer generation is server-authoritative.** It runs only inside the sandboxed authority module,
    against the injected RNG, and ships to clients in the state snapshot — the same rule that governs
    the per-era turn-order shuffle. No RNG-derived logic may run on a client mirror. The sandbox has
    no `Date`, no `fetch` and no DOM.
*   **The dictionary never reaches the client in multiplayer.** The platform's word service already
    exposes `count`, `pick`, `countOfLength` and `pickOfLength`, which is sufficient to build a
    length-shaped Offer host-side.
*   **No per-recipient state is required.** Because the Offer is public and turns are sequential, the
    authority stays in broadcast mode; the platform's `perRecipient` projection is not needed.
*   **Two new intents** are required: a *select* intent (mirroring the existing draft-word intent, and
    throttled the same way) and a *commit* intent (mirroring submit). The sender's player id is
    already established by the transport, so turn-order spoofing is handled by existing checks.
*   **Bots.** The ranking half of the existing scored bot chooser is already exactly *"score this
    candidate set through my bay and take the best"* — extract it as a shared function. Only the
    gathering half, which walks the dictionary by starting letter, is replaced by the Offer. Bots
    exist only in the local controller; the server seeds no bots.
*   **Settings plumbing.** Each new setting touches, in order: the settings interface → the defaults
    table → the validators (out-of-range values **reject to default, never clamp**) → the stored
    settings version, which must be **bumped from 3 to 4** or stale saved blobs will load → the
    settings hint copy → both the solo and networked lobby views.
*   **Deal pool.** Disabled cards (§4.4) must be filtered out of the dealable pool per mode. The deal
    capacity helpers that back the lobby's *"enabled rarity tiers cannot cover this many deals"*
    warning are computed from pool size and will be wrong unless they read the mode-filtered pool.
*   **Player-facing copy.** The build's README describes typed play as the game; it needs updating
    alongside, since Picker becomes what a new player meets first (§1.1).
*   **Dictionary shipping.** The Reduced list is not currently packaged. It needs copying into the
    client assets, a tier concept added to the client dictionary loader (which today exposes only
    membership, size and a starting-letter index), and a second entry in the game manifest's
    authority word list.

## 8. Implementation Deviations and Open Questions

*   **The base GDD's card catalogue lags the target build.** `alpha-chain-gdd.md` §3 documents **40**
    cards; the Phaser build ships **47**. Cards documented there but pruned from the build include the
    Titanium Mirror (and the whole shield system), the Flak Cannon, the Bounty Hunter, the Anchor
    Chain and Hyper-Drive; cards in the build but absent from the base GDD include Stonemason,
    Numismatist, The Flywheel, The Lexicon, Loan Shark, The Sniper, **Insurance**, Tilesmith,
    Crescendo, Bookends and Dividend. Several shared cards also carry different numbers — the
    Blindfold is **×1.5** rather than ×1.8, Redline costs **−30%** clock rather than −20%, and the
    Vault **−20%** rather than −10%. **Where this document names values,
    they are the build's.** The base GDD should be reconciled separately; that work is out of scope
    here, but Picker's balance passes will be reading the build, not the base document.
*   **No timeout penalty in Picker.** The base timeout penalty and every card's timeout drain are
    inert (§2.4). This is a deliberate departure from base §6's framing of a timeout as a punishment,
    and it is why Survival keys on a **no-show** instead.
*   **The Zero-Point Tax loses its tactical-pass role** (§4.2). Accepted, traded for the unmarked
    poisoned word.
*   **Open — the Picker shot clock default.** Playtest.
*   **Open — the default Offer Dictionary.** Full favours variety and repeat play; Reduced favours
    decodability and is the accessibility-first answer. Since Picker is the default mode, this default
    decides what most players experience. Playtest.
*   **Open — the unsatisfiable-filter threshold** (§3.2). "Skip any filter that would drop the pool
    below the Offer count" is the stated rule; whether a partially-satisfiable filter should instead
    apply to a subset of the Offer is untested.
*   **Open — Preference Card rarity and deal weights.** The seven cards in §3.4 have no assigned
    rarities. Because they compete with scoring cards for slots, their deal rate directly controls how
    often the mode's central dilemma is actually posed.
